# AI Writer Research Pass 4
## Page-Level Craft, Common Writer Mistakes, and Storytelling Questions
### Version 0.4 research synthesis

## Why this pass exists

AI Writer v0.3 already has strong systems for:
- character state;
- agency;
- continuity;
- suspense;
- curiosity;
- surprise;
- dynamic outlining;
- reader-state modeling;
- StoryScope-inspired anti-default checks.

Its comparatively thin layer was **page-level craft execution**:
- when to show versus tell;
- where a scene should begin/end;
- how to control psychic distance;
- how to reveal backstory;
- how dialogue carries subtext;
- how common novice mistakes appear on the page.

v0.4 treats these as separate, diagnosable systems.

---

# 1. "Show, Don't Tell" Is Better as "Choose Experience vs Compression"

Multiple current craft sources make the same correction:

```text
SHOW
when the reader should live through the moment

TELL / SUMMARY
when the reader mainly needs information or compression
```

Jane Friedman contributors and Reedsy educators explicitly note that telling is necessary for:
- transitions;
- summary;
- pacing;
- interior voice;
- context.

This leads to the v0.4 Show–Tell Decision Engine.

Important anti-overcorrection:
StoryScope reports that AI fiction can overperform embodied/sensory writing. Therefore "showing" must not become a machine that converts every emotion into heartbeat/chest/breath/jaw imagery.

---

# 2. Scene = Change Under Pressure

Reedsy's scene-craft material emphasizes:
- character goal;
- conflict/resistance;
- stakes;
- momentum;
- change.

v0.4 therefore asks scene-level questions rather than relying on a generic "good scene" score.

Core diagnostic:

```text
Who wants what?
What resists?
What changes?
Why does the next scene now have to be different?
```

---

# 3. Enter Late, Leave Early Is a Revision Test, Not a Religion

Multiple editors/teachers identify a common beginner problem:
- warming up too long before the interesting scene;
- lingering after the scene's real turn to explain/recap.

The principle is useful as a cutting test.

But v0.4 explicitly protects:
- dread;
- ritual;
- slice-of-life;
- atmosphere;
- awkward duration;
- grief;
- slow-burn scenes.

Slowness is valid when duration creates the effect.

---

# 4. Backstory Should Answer a Live Reader Question

Jane Friedman contributor Susan DeFreitas outlines several useful exposition tactics:
- show;
- show then tell;
- make backstory its own story;
- withhold backstory as reveal.

The strongest generalization for an agent is:

```text
reader experiences present scene
→ question becomes live
→ release the smallest useful context
```

This prevents encyclopedia-first worldbuilding.

---

# 5. Psychic Distance Is a Craft Variable

University of Nevada, Reno's Writing & Speaking Center summarizes John Gardner's psychic-distance model as the felt distance between reader and event.

Larger distance is useful for:
- context;
- large arcs;
- compression.

Closer distance is useful for:
- important scene detail;
- voice;
- interior experience.

v0.4 converts this into:
- wide;
- medium;
- close;
- immersed.

The goal is deliberate zoom rather than accidental distance drift.

---

# 6. Dialogue Needs an Unsaid Layer

Current dialogue craft guidance repeatedly warns against:
- dialogue used as exposition;
- characters directly stating every feeling;
- characters reciting shared history;
- identical character voices.

v0.4 adds:
- surface topic;
- real topic;
- hidden sentence;
- tactic;
- deflection;
- social cost;
- meaningful silence.

Direct dialogue remains legal when directness itself is the dramatic event.

---

# 7. Common Novice Problems Are Often Selection Problems

Editors/teachers repeatedly identify:
- generic language;
- wordiness;
- laundry-list description;
- too much exposition;
- POV confusion/head-hopping;
- unnatural dialogue;
- weak specificity.

Many of these can be reframed as failure to select:

```text
too many details
too many explanations
too many emotional labels
too much background
too many equal-weight moments
```

Strong fiction often chooses fewer details with higher function.

---

# 8. Stakes Are Personal Before They Are Large

Writing educators repeatedly define stakes through:
- what the character wants;
- why;
- what blocks them;
- what they lose;
- what action costs.

The agent should not inflate tension by defaulting to world-ending stakes.

"End of the character's world" is often stronger than "end of the world."

---

# 9. Setup / Payoff Should Track Reader Attention

Setup/payoff guidance is useful, but can become mechanical.

v0.4 tracks only conspicuous promises:
- object emphasized;
- question foregrounded;
- capability established;
- threat planted;
- relationship rule repeated.

Ordinary texture need not pay off.

---

# 10. StoryScope Remains a Critical Anti-Default Layer

StoryScope (Russell et al., 2026) compared 61,608 stories and found discourse-level differences between human and AI fiction.

Important defaults for v0.4:
- AI over-explains themes;
- AI favors tidy, single-track plots;
- AI more often resolves through protagonist-centered closure;
- humans show more moral ambiguity and temporal complexity;
- human-authored stories occupy a wider narrative feature space.

These findings should NOT be inverted mechanically.

Do not force:
- flashbacks;
- moral ambiguity;
- subplots;
- messy causality.

Use them as red flags when a draft feels suspiciously frictionless.

---

# 11. The New Runtime Shape

```text
INTENT
↓
STORY / CHARACTER DESIGN
↓
SCENE QUESTION PASS
↓
SCENE CONTRACT
↓
SHOW–TELL DECISION
↓
POV / PSYCHIC DISTANCE
↓
DIALOGUE / SUBTEXT
↓
DRAFT
↓
CRAFT AUDIT
↓
STATE / CONTINUITY
↓
SPECIALIZED CRITICS
↓
REVISION PASSES
```

The important change is that "prose" is no longer one undifferentiated stage.
