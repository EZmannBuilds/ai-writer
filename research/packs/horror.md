# Research: Horror / Dread

Pack: [packs/horror.md](../../packs/horror.md). Research pass 2026-09-16.

**Transfer caution.** Much of the empirical work studies film viewers, haunted-attraction visitors, or clinical anxiety. Applying it to prose readers is an analogy; those entries are marked approximate or conceptual.

**Coverage gap.** No credible dedicated source was found for isolation, social disbelief, or aftermath as a narrative phase. The pack labels that guidance craft inference.

## Empirical

### `andersen-2020`

- **Source:** Andersen, Schjoedt, Price, Rosas, Scrivner & Clasen, "Playing With Fear: A Field Study in Recreational Horror," *Psychological Science* 31(12), 1497–1510, 2020. https://pure.au.dk/portal/en/publications/playing-with-fear-a-field-study-in-recreational-horror/
- **Type:** empirical
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16 (repository record)
- **Claim:** In 110 haunted-attraction visitors with heart-rate monitors, enjoyment had an inverted-U relation to self-reported fear, and small heart-rate fluctuations related to enjoyment in an inverted U — a "just right" zone of arousal dynamics.
- **Derives:** The dread curve: aim for a sweet spot, not maximum fear; alternate tension and release.
- **Limitations:** One live attraction, not prose; modest sample.
- **Fidelity:** approximate — a physical, physiological finding applied to reading by analogy.

### `clasen-2020`

- **Source:** Clasen, Kjeldgaard-Christiansen & Johnson, "Horror, Personality, and Threat Simulation: A Survey on the Psychology of Scary Media," *Evolutionary Behavioral Sciences* 14(3), 213–230, 2020. https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.psu.edu/dist/1/163537/files/2023/07/ebs_14_3_213.pdf
- **Type:** empirical
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16 (full text)
- **Claim:** In 1,070 respondents, sensation seeking and intellect/imagination predicted liking horror; people with stronger paranormal beliefs preferred supernatural horror. Fans expect positive as well as negative emotions.
- **Derives:** Match the supernatural or naturalistic frame to the audience; horror readers want intellectual engagement and rehearsal of coping.
- **Limitations:** Cross-sectional online self-report.
- **Fidelity:** conceptual

### `scrivner-2021`

- **Source:** Scrivner, Johnson, Kjeldgaard-Christiansen & Clasen, "Pandemic practice: Horror fans and morbidly curious individuals are more psychologically resilient during the COVID-19 pandemic," *Personality and Individual Differences* 168, 110397, 2021. https://pure.au.dk/portal/en/publications/93a029b5-ae69-472b-a238-a2fb555c56fb
- **Type:** empirical
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16 (repository record)
- **Claim:** Horror fans reported more pandemic resilience; the authors propose frightening fiction as coping practice.
- **Derives:** Show characters coping, including failing; counterplay as part of the appeal.
- **Limitations:** Correlational self-report; cannot show horror causes resilience.
- **Fidelity:** conceptual

### `martin-2019`

- **Source:** G. Neil Martin, "(Why) Do You Like Scary Movies? A Review of the Empirical Research on Psychological Responses to Horror Films," *Frontiers in Psychology* 10, 2298, 2019. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6813198/
- **Type:** empirical
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16
- **Claim:** Enjoyment predictors are inconsistent and content-dependent; many viewers report lingering fear; relief-at-resolution (excitation transfer) has only limited support.
- **Derives:** Aftermath as a real phase; unresolved endings are legitimate.
- **Limitations:** Review of film research with varied, small, mostly non-longitudinal studies.
- **Fidelity:** conceptual

## Theory

### `grupe-nitschke-2013`

- **Source:** Grupe & Nitschke, "Uncertainty and anticipation in anxiety: an integrated neurobiological and psychological perspective," *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* 14, 488–501, 2013. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4276319/
- **Type:** theoretical
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16
- **Claim:** Uncertainty about future threat drives anxiety through inflated threat estimates, hypervigilance, poor safety learning, avoidance, and heightened reactivity to uncertainty; unpredictably timed aversive events have larger effects.
- **Derives:** Uncertainty as its own dial; varying *when*; characters who can't learn when they're safe; rule beliefs that fail.
- **Limitations:** Clinical anxiety research; applying it to readers is an analogy.
- **Fidelity:** conceptual

### `clasen-2017`

- **Source:** Mathias Clasen, *Why Horror Seduces*, Oxford University Press, 2017. https://academic.oup.com/book/4159
- **Type:** theoretical
- **Status:** found by search; publisher page not opened
- **Claim:** Horror targets evolved fear mechanisms, and audiences enjoy negative emotion inside a safe frame.
- **Derives:** Background for the safe-frame assumption.
- **Limitations:** Not read; chapter-level claims unverified.
- **Fidelity:** conceptual — not relied on for specific rules.

### `carroll-1990`

- **Source:** Noël Carroll, *The Philosophy of Horror, or Paradoxes of the Heart*, Routledge, 1990. https://www.routledge.com/The-Philosophy-of-Horror-Or-Paradoxes-of-the-Heart/Carroll/p/book/9780415902168
- **Type:** theoretical
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16 (publisher page); content via secondary summaries
- **Claim:** Art-horror responds to beings judged both threatening and impure; impurity comes from category violation (interstitial, contradictory, incomplete, formless), with fusion and fission among its monster types.
- **Derives:** Threats must be dangerous and wrong; name the category violated; contamination.
- **Limitations:** Philosophical; content claims from secondary sources; contested.
- **Fidelity:** conceptual

### `freud-1919`

- **Source:** Sigmund Freud, "The 'Uncanny'," 1919 (Alix Strachey translation). https://web.mit.edu/allanmc/www/freud1.pdf
- **Type:** theoretical
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16 (text extracted)
- **Claim:** The uncanny leads back to the once-familiar; openly fantastical settings are not uncanny, while fiction set in apparent reality can use real-life uncanny effects; keeping readers unsure which rules apply sustains it.
- **Derives:** Safety baseline; rule uncertainty; revealing rules reduces the uncanny.
- **Limitations:** Psychoanalytic theory without empirical support.
- **Fidelity:** conceptual

### `mori-1970`

- **Source:** Masahiro Mori, "The Uncanny Valley," 1970; authorized English translation by MacDorman & Kageki, *IEEE Spectrum* / *IEEE Robotics & Automation Magazine* 19(2), 2012. https://spectrum.ieee.org/the-uncanny-valley
- **Type:** theoretical
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16
- **Claim:** Affinity rises with human likeness, then drops sharply before full likeness; movement amplifies the effect.
- **Derives:** Near-human wrongness shown through motion and behavior.
- **Limitations:** A speculative essay about robots; later empirical tests are mixed (not checked here).
- **Fidelity:** conceptual

### `cruz-2012`

- **Source:** Ronald Allan L. Cruz, "Mutations and Metamorphoses: Body Horror is Biological Horror," *Journal of Popular Film and Television* 40(4), 160–168, 2012. https://archium.ateneo.edu/biology-faculty-pubs/55/
- **Type:** theoretical
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16 (abstract)
- **Claim:** Body horror — graphic violation of the body through hybrids, metamorphoses, mutation, and zombification — can be called biological horror.
- **Derives:** Body horror as its own mode.
- **Limitations:** Abstract only; film criticism.
- **Fidelity:** conceptual

### `acosta-sears-2025`

- **Source:** Acosta & Sears, "(Re)Considering the jump scare in four elements," *Frontiers in Psychology* (opinion), doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1569394. https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1569394/full
- **Type:** theoretical
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16; publication date on the page and DOI year differ — confirm before citing a year
- **Claim:** Jump scares are aesthetic experiences shaped by anticipatory cues and aftermath; abandoned stingers (build, no payoff) differ from unprompted ones (no warning).
- **Derives:** Shocks need a frame; the false alarm and the unwarned shock are different tools.
- **Limitations:** Opinion piece; film and audio.
- **Fidelity:** conceptual

## Historical and craft

### `radcliffe-1826`

- **Source:** Ann Radcliffe, "On the Supernatural in Poetry," *New Monthly Magazine* 16, 1826 (written c. 1802–03). https://resources.saylor.org/wwwresources/archived/site/wp-content/uploads/2011/01/On-the-Supernatural-in-Poetry.pdf
- **Type:** historical-primary
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16
- **Claim:** A speaker in the dialogue distinguishes terror, which expands the faculties, from horror, which contracts them, locating the difference in terror's uncertainty and obscurity.
- **Derives:** Terror before horror; the visibility ladder; obscurity versus confusion.
- **Limitations:** An aesthetic opinion voiced by a fictional speaker.
- **Fidelity:** convention

### `lovecraft-1927`

- **Source:** H. P. Lovecraft, "Supernatural Horror in Literature," 1927 (revised later). https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/essays/shil.aspx
- **Type:** historical-primary
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16
- **Claim:** Fear of the unknown is the oldest and strongest fear; atmosphere matters most; the test of a weird tale is whether it excites dread and a sense of contact with unknown spheres.
- **Derives:** The cosmic mode's success test.
- **Limitations:** One author's manifesto, reflecting his canon and prejudices.
- **Fidelity:** convention

### `tanen-2024`

- **Source:** Alexa Tanen, "Halloween Haunts: Evoking Dread: A Balancing Act," Horror Writers Association blog, 2024-10-09. https://horror.org/halloween-haunts-evoking-dread-a-balancing-act/
- **Type:** craft-practice
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16
- **Claim:** Balance what's revealed and withheld, how much is described, and slow build against fast shock.
- **Derives:** Practitioner agreement with the visibility and pacing rules.
- **Limitations:** One practitioner's blog post, not HWA policy.
- **Fidelity:** convention

### `waggoner-2020`

- **Source:** Tim Waggoner, "Done to Death: How to Avoid Horror Genre Clichés," *Writer's Digest*, 2020-09-30. https://www.writersdigest.com/write-better-fiction/done-to-death
- **Type:** craft-practice
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16
- **Claim:** Lists overused material (vampires, witches, ghosts, zombies, clowns, the dead narrator) and advises rejecting first ideas and combining concepts.
- **Derives:** The anti-default audit's overused-shell list and the fresh-angle requirement.
- **Limitations:** Lists defaults without explaining why they fail.
- **Fidelity:** convention
