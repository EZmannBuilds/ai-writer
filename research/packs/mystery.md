# Research: Mystery / Investigation

Pack: [packs/mystery.md](../../packs/mystery.md). Research pass 2026-09-16; every URL was fetched.

Also used, with entries elsewhere: [`loewenstein-1994`](../core/reader-psychology.md#loewenstein-1994), [`brewer-lichtenstein-1982`](../core/reader-psychology.md#brewer-lichtenstein-1982), [`narrative-surprise`](../core/reader-psychology.md#narrative-surprise).

**Cautions from the pass.**

- **Spoiler research is contested.** Don't design as if a mystery's value lay only in secrecy, and don't design as if spoilers never matter.
- **Detection Club dates conflict** across sources (founded 1930 per the archive holding its records; an Open University course says Knox led it in 1928). The claim that Knox's rules became club bylaws appears only on secondary pages.
- **Historical rules are conventions** that many writers break on purpose, and two of them are prejudiced. The pack does not apply those.

## Empirical and theoretical

### `hoeken-vanvliet-2000`

- **Source:** Hoeken & van Vliet, "Suspense, curiosity, and surprise: How discourse structure influences the affective and cognitive processing of a story," *Poetics* 27(4), 277–286, 2000. doi:10.1016/S0304-422X(99)00021-2
- **Type:** empirical
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16 (bibliographic details only; the article text could not be loaded)
- **Claim:** Per search summaries, varies the event order of a detective story to test Brewer & Lichtenstein's structural-affect theory.
- **Derives:** Supports treating discourse order as a lever for suspense, curiosity, and surprise — pending a check of the actual results.
- **Limitations:** Findings unverified; likely a student lab sample.
- **Fidelity:** conceptual — not relied on until checked.

### `leavitt-christenfeld-2011`

- **Source:** Leavitt & Christenfeld, "Story Spoilers Don't Spoil Stories," *Psychological Science* 22(9), 1152–1154, 2011. APS press release: https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/spoiler-alert-stories-are-not-spoiled-byspoilers.html
- **Type:** empirical
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16 (press release; paper not opened)
- **Claim:** Across three experiments with ironic-twist, mystery, and literary stories, readers liked spoiled versions more.
- **Derives:** A mystery's pleasure shouldn't rest entirely on the answer being secret.
- **Limitations:** Disputed by later work; the press release and a later paper describe the spoiler presentation differently; short stories only.
- **Fidelity:** conceptual

### `levine-raines-2025`

- **Source:** Levine & Raines, "The Role of Different Spoiler Types in the Experience of Short Stories," *Scientific Study of Literature* 14(1), 27–44, 2025. https://ssol-journal.com/articles/190/files/6818ab3f892e8.pdf
- **Type:** empirical
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16 (full text read)
- **Claim:** With 326 students and twist-ending stories, neither theme spoilers nor plot spoilers much changed enjoyment; readers who enjoy effortful thinking had better experiences; readers high in need for closure felt more suspense. The authors call the 2011 result an outlier.
- **Derives:** Serve both solvers and answer-seekers; early in-story information behaves more like foreshadowing than a spoiler.
- **Limitations:** College students; literary twist stories, not full mysteries.
- **Fidelity:** conceptual

### `foster-keane-2013`

- **Source:** Foster & Keane, "Surprise: You've got some explaining to do," Proceedings of CogSci 2013. https://arxiv.org/abs/1308.2236
- **Type:** empirical
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16
- **Claim:** Surprise tracks how hard an outcome is to explain; explaining an outcome lowers its rated surprise.
- **Derives:** Fairness test 4: hard to explain beforehand, easy afterward; the reconstruction delivers the ease.
- **Limitations:** Short scenario texts; lab participants; measures surprise, not enjoyment.
- **Fidelity:** conceptual

### `wagner-fairplay-2025`

- **Source:** Wagner, Keydar & Abend, "The Challenge and Reward of Fair Play in Narrative: A Computational Approach," arXiv 2507.13841, 2025 (revised 2026; preprint). https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.13841
- **Type:** empirical
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16; preprint
- **Claim:** Surprise and coherence conflict for a single reader model but can coexist across two reading modes — expectation before the reveal and re-reading after it; LLM-generated stories rarely achieved both.
- **Derives:** Fairness tests 2 and 3: a misled reader and an ideal reader.
- **Limitations:** Preprint; language models stand in for readers; human-study details come from a summary.
- **Fidelity:** approximate — the two readers are simulated by reasoning, not by the paper's probability model.

### `tobin-2018`

- **Source:** Vera Tobin, *Elements of Surprise: Our Mental Limits and the Satisfactions of Plot*, Harvard University Press, 2018. https://veratobin.org/publications/elements-of-surprise/
- **Type:** theoretical
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16 (book description only)
- **Claim:** Well-made surprises exploit readers' cognitive biases and memory quirks; they are skilled craft, not cheap tricks.
- **Derives:** Misdirection built on attention and memory rather than on hiding facts.
- **Limitations:** Only the description was read; humanities and cognitive analysis, not new experiments.
- **Fidelity:** conceptual

### `tobin-conversation-2018`

- **Source:** Vera Tobin, "The science of the plot twist – how writers exploit our brains," *The Conversation*, 2018-05-11. https://theconversation.com/the-science-of-the-plot-twist-how-writers-exploit-our-brains-95748
- **Type:** theoretical
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16
- **Claim:** The curse of knowledge shapes what readers notice; red herrings anchor false expectations; a twist's deepest pleasure is seeing earlier details gain new meaning; twists from nowhere hurt.
- **Derives:** Two meanings per clue; testing fairness with a fresh reader model because the author's knowledge distorts judgment.
- **Limitations:** Popular-science article; film examples.
- **Fidelity:** conceptual

## Historical conventions

### `van-dine-1928`

- **Source:** S. S. Van Dine, "Twenty Rules for Writing Detective Stories," *The American Magazine*, September 1928 (transcription). https://www.staff.u-szeged.hu/~gnovak/vandine.htm
- **Type:** historical-primary
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16 (transcription)
- **Claim:** The reader should have an equal chance to solve the case; clues must be plainly stated; the culprit must be prominent; plus rules many modern mysteries ignore (no love interest) and a class-bound servant rule.
- **Derives:** The equal-opportunity principle and the culprit-presence test. Prejudiced and dated rules are excluded.
- **Limitations:** Opinionated 1928 essay; transcription not checked against the magazine.
- **Fidelity:** convention

### `knox-1929`

- **Source:** Ronald Knox, "Ten Commandments" of detective fiction, 1929, as reproduced by novelist Elizabeth Spann Craig. https://elizabethspanncraig.com/mystery-writing-tips/ten-commandments-detective-fiction-1929-brief-history/
- **Type:** historical-primary
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16 (secondary reproduction)
- **Claim:** Rules including: the criminal appears early; no supernatural solution; no undiscovered poisons; no solution by luck or intuition; the reader sees every clue; twins only if prepared. One rule is an ethnic stereotype.
- **Derives:** Clue accessibility, culprit presence, and the generalized rule that solutions use only established world rules.
- **Limitations:** No primary facsimile found; written partly in jest; one rule is racist and excluded.
- **Fidelity:** convention

### `detection-club-oath`

- **Source:** Gladstone's Library, "The Records of the Detection Club." https://www.gladstoneslibrary.org/reading-rooms/the-collections/archives/the-records-of-the-detection-club/
- **Type:** historical-primary
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16
- **Claim:** The club was founded in 1930; members swear an oath that their detectives will truly detect, without divine revelation, intuition, coincidence, or acts of God (paraphrased).
- **Derives:** Fairness test 7: reasoning solves the case, not chance.
- **Limitations:** Ceremonial and partly comic; wording has changed over time; founding date disputed.
- **Fidelity:** convention

### `freeman-1912`

- **Source:** R. Austin Freeman, preface to *The Singing Bone* (stories 1912; Project Gutenberg text of a 1923 edition). https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/59478/pg59478-images.html
- **Type:** historical-primary
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16
- **Claim:** Freeman argues the investigation interests clever readers more than the answer, and describes inverted stories where the reader knows everything and the detective nothing.
- **Derives:** The inverted (howcatchem) form.
- **Limitations:** An author defending his own method.
- **Fidelity:** convention

## Craft and teaching

### `openlearn-golden-age`

- **Source:** The Open University (OpenLearn), "Agatha Christie and the golden age of detective fiction," section 1. https://www.open.edu/openlearn/history-the-arts/agatha-christie-and-the-golden-age-detective-fiction/content-section-1
- **Type:** craft-practice
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16
- **Claim:** Fair play as a game with the reader defines the genre; the puzzle should be hard but logically reachable; *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd* was attacked and defended over the rules.
- **Derives:** Fair play as a contract; convention-breaking can still be fair.
- **Limitations:** Introductory teaching material; its Detection Club date conflicts with the archive.
- **Fidelity:** convention

### `calvin-2021`

- **Source:** Kris Calvin, "Planting Clues: Red Herrings That Fool but Don't Frustrate Your Readers," *Writer's Digest*, 2021-04-15. https://www.writersdigest.com/write-better-fiction/planting-clues-red-herrings-that-fool-but-dont-frustrate-your-readers
- **Type:** craft-practice
- **Status:** verified 2026-09-16
- **Claim:** Give each significant character a secret with its own reason; not every red herring needs resolving on the page; don't introduce the killer late; track herrings after drafting.
- **Derives:** Red herrings from real secrets; the resolved-on-page vs backstory flag.
- **Limitations:** One practitioner; aimed at traditional and cozy mysteries.
- **Fidelity:** convention
